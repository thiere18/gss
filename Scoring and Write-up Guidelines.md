The purpose of the write-up is to report on the performance of the candidate based on what occurred during the interview. ## Scores and Recommendations 

The score is used to place candidates in one of four **Karat recommendation categories** : 

- Fast track to next round 

- Invite to next round 

- Requires further review 

- Do not pursue 



The scoring thresholds for each bucket are specific and carefully adjusted for each client. 

Clients use the score and Karat Recommendation as factors in the decision to advance or decline the candidate within their hiring process. 



Different clients will have different policies with regards to scores. For example, some clients may read all write ups to look for a diamond in the rough, and some may ignore all candidates in "Requires further review". 

Because scoring has such an **impact on whether someone gets hired** , it is vital that Karat provides **consistent results** . This consistency is also what sets Karat apart from many internal interviewing teams. 

For any answer that is at your discretion (such as an extra credit), if the discretionary credit is given, you must leave a QC note and request a QC review.

### Grammar 

Many Interview Engineers use a service called Grammarly that is installed as a browser extension. It can alert you of common grammatical mistakes and assist you in proofreading as you write. 

Here are some general grammatical guidelines to follow: 

- Write in third person. Use either "the interviewer" to refer to yourself, or reword to avoid adding yourself as a subject at all. 

   - "I prompted him to stop looking after defining the rectangle." (Incorrect) 

   - "The interviewer prompted him to stop looking after defining the rectangle." (Correct) 

   - "He was prompted to stop looking after defining the rectangle." (Correct) 

Make sure to use the right indefinite articles 

   - a -> an 

   - Forgetting the word "the" 

   - & -> and 

- Verbs should be in past tense, in line with describing facts that happened, rather than generalizations about how strong a candidate is overall. 

   - "is quick" -> "was quick" 

   - "understand" -> "understood" 

Avoid incomplete sentences 

### Guidelines 

**Do not use "was able", "was not able"** or **could (not) do** when describing what the candidate did or did not do. We don’t speak about the abilities of the candidate in general, only about what the candidate actually did or did not do in the interview. 

- When describing the approach of the candidate, **do not say that the candidate "came up with" an approach.** This wording tends to diminish the accomplishment of the candidate. You can alternatively use "formulated", "constructed", "identified", or any other formal wording to describe the process of working out the approach or choosing test cases. 

- All numbers less than 10 should be written out as words instead of numerals, eg. one, two, three instead of 1, 2, 3. Numbers greater than or equal to 10 can be written out as words or as numerals. 

- Please use "the interviewer" to refer to yourself rather than speaking from the first person perspective with the use of "I" or "we." 

- Refer to the candidate by first name rather than "the candidate." 

- Please ensure that your writing contains complete sentences. Make sure that you include the subject in each sentence. Bullet points and numbered lists are fine as long as they are professional and easy to understand. 

- Please make use of single backticks ( \` ) to surround the inclusion of specific types, method names, Big-O notation or other items that should be represented as highlighted monospace text. This is a feature of Markdown. 

- Do not be concerned about your report being original or novel from one report to the next. 

- When making reference to a specific dimension of scoring, like completeness, approach quality, or handholding, be sure to use the same terms that are used in the form. 

   - For example, instead of writing it like this -- 

      - "Billy wrote a nearly complete solution for the first coding question." 

   - Write it using the proper scoring terminology: 

      - "Billy wrote a mostly working solution for the first coding question." 

- Always use the past tense where possible. 

   - This includes projects that are currently ongoing in the project discussion. 

- Avoid repetition 

   - E.g., you don’t have to write down the approach if you’ve selected an approach E.g., you don’t have to state the project was of "Average Difficulty" if already marked. (Unless you’re about to explain why you marked the  difficulty as average, and you need to mention it to set the context for your explanation.) 

- When making reference to a particular coding question, refer to it as "the first coding question", "the first coding problem" or similar, as appropriate. Avoid "Question One", "question one" and alike. 

- Avoid words such as "very", "really", "extremely", and so on. In most instances, these words can simply be deleted. They are vague descriptors that don’t add to the write-up. 

- Use formal language. Avoid incorrect capitalization and incorrect spelling of technologies -- eg write JavaScript, MongoDB, etc. -- if you’re unsure, look it up! Avoid vague statements, and words such as "some", which is defined as "being of an unspecified amount or number". 

- Avoid colloquialism 

- Be careful about calling code ["idiomatic"](#idiomatic-code) if you aren't **certain** it is 

- Keep in mind that the client and other people reviewing the candidate’s profile may not have watched the video, and may only have a few minutes to review the profile. Set context for information that is not covered by the default form fields. A client should be able to get the full picture just from reading the scores you entered and the text you wrote. For example, if the candidate switched approaches mid-way through a coding problem, don’t just say "Her second approach was better and resulted in a working solution." You don’t need to be overly verbose, but add some context: "Her initial approach of using exclusively goto statements was not working well. She switched to using a nested for loop instead, which resulted in a working solution."

### Idiomatic Code 

Be careful about calling code that you observed in the interview "idiomatic" in your writing. 

"Idiomatic" code refers to a specific, opinionated style that usually comes after working with focus and close integration with the community and ecosystem of a particular coding language. 

Just because certain code is "clean" or "readable" does not make it idiomatic. If you call code idiomatic mistakenly, your credibility in assessing code in that language could be called into question by a hiring manager that is more aware and cognizant of what is classified as "idiomatic" in that language. 

For more information, see this blog post.

### Project Discussion: Scoring 

Scoring for project section is difficult and subjective. We rate the **Difficulty** of the project and the **Understanding** . 

We expect that a strong candidate has worked on something difficult and they have a strong understanding of that project. 

We rate Level of Difficulty and Level of Understanding independently. The available scores for each of these are: 

- Minimal 

- Moderate Above average Significant 

We also have a "N/A Not enough info" option for if the project was not discussed, or if the candidate's explanation was extremely difficult to understand. This should be the case only exceptionally, and you should make it clear in the project description and QC notes why you picked this option. 

This lesson provides a set of examples of project scores, and Interview Engineers are 

expected to map each interview to the closest of these examples in terms of difficulty and understanding. 

### Project Discussion: Level of Difficulty 

Nothing in life is inherently easy or difficult. Tasks are intrinsically simple or complex, and difficulty is a product of experience and the complexity of the task. We are scoring based on how complex the project compared to other projects was and not how hard it was for the candidate. That is, creating a basic CRUD site is hard for a beginner, and creating an image recognition tool may be easy for an image recognition expert, but in our scoring system the latter is scored as harder than the former. 

Projects can be difficult in multiple ways. For example: 

- The scope of the project is large and requires a lot of domain knowledge. 

- The project pushes some boundaries of what is possible with existing tools. 

- The project is done to a rigorous standard with a high requirement for correctness or performance. 

We are scoring mainly based on the technical difficulty of the project. That means that a simple to implement project cannot be marked as "Significant" difficulty if it involved dealing with team bureaucracy or unprofessional colleagues. 

### Level of Difficulty Options 

#### Minimal 

A project with a minimal level of difficulty is basic. It could be completed by someone with a low level of technical experience or skill, typically by piecing together a few libraries or tools. 

Examples include: 

- Creating a basic CRUD website 

- Doing a school project 

- Playing a small part in a complex project 

- Converting an AngularJS component to React 

- Creating a website/game in a short amount of time at a coding bootcamp 

- Using an existing machine learning model or library to categorize images 

#### Moderate 

A project that presented some obstacles is scored as moderately difficult. Most projects out in industry fall into this category. 

Examples include: 

- Working on part of a team creating an API to an existing database 

- Adding tests to an existing service 

- Developing a machine learning pipeline that trains a model for use in a production system. 

#### Above Average 

A challenging project that involves collaboration with multiple disciplines is of above average difficulty. 

Examples include: 

- Writing a thoroughly tested trading system which is very performant 

- Implementing a permissions system for a new service 

- Creating an ETL system that processes very large volumes of data 

- Creating and deploying a novel machine learning model 

#### Significant 

Significantly difficult projects solve a problem that most people could not solve. This is a rare choice and indicates a special candidate. 

For example: 

- Modifying a popular programming language in a significant way 

- Handling an unusually large scale in terms of users or data 

- Making a game with a technically novel mechanic 

### Project Discussion: Level of Understanding 

Understanding of a project can be demonstrated in multiple way. A candidate may show a deep understanding of the business requirements of a project, or the technical stack used, or the technical problem space. 

A strong candidate will show an understanding of the user or client needs, the technical choices made and the impact that these had. 

### Project Level of Understanding Options 

#### Minimal (Project) 

A candidate knows only the basics of what was implemented, if at all. They do not know where the project fits into the bigger picture. They do not have an understanding of the reasons for technical decisions or the impact of these decisions. 

#### Moderate (Project) 

The candidate can explain the project's use from end to end. They may not know all of the technical details of other components, especially if it was a team project. They may not have been involved in key decisions. 

#### Above average (Project) 

The candidate provided information about why certain technologies were used. This may include the programming language and infrastructure selections such as the database. They understand technical details of other components that interface with this project. 

This candidate may also demonstrate domain expertise such as expertise in image recognition or distributed systems. They may also demonstrate business context knowledge such as a deep understanding of the users' needs. 

#### Significant (Project) 

The candidate understands where the project fits into the larger picture. 

They show a deep understanding of the technical decisions and intricacies of the project. They understand the technical and business value that the project brings to the company. The candidate understands some client issues, operational issues, and the future roadmap of the project. 

They understand the requirements of the project in dimensions such as correctness, performance and features. 

#### N/A Not enough info 

This would happen if the project was not discussed at all.

Some candidates provide a link to showcase their work. As these are outside of Karat control, they may pose a security risk, so only include them if you are **certain** they point to well-known sites such as gitlab.com or github.com. If in doubt, omit them.

## Programming Problems: Scoring 

The strongest candidates 

- Write fully working solutions 

- Require no help from the Interview Engineer 

- Choose approaches which are optimal in terms of time and space complexities Know the time and space complexities of their approaches 

Clients also want to know if the candidate thoroughly planned their approach. 

## Programming Problems: Optimality of Approach 

The approach explains how the candidate solved the problem. Most approaches to a problem have already been identified with their corresponding optimality in the question guide. 

Each question guide includes multiple approaches for each question (ex. Q1, Q2, Q3). Each of these have an associated time and space complexities. The approaches are ranked between: 

###### **No viable approach** 

###### **Naive** 

###### **Acceptable** 

###### **Optimal** 

Match the candidate's approach with an approach in the question guide. **Both the textual description and the complexity of the implementation should match.** 

Note: Some questions have optimality cut-offs or ranges. Eg: Anything above a certain time/space complexity is considered optimal, anything below a certain complexity is considered naive, and anything between two complexities is considered acceptable. Choose the correct bucket for these ranges if this is the case. 

On rare occasions the candidate's approach will not match any of the approaches in the guide. In these cases, compute the time and space complexities of the solution and compare it against all solutions in the question guide. If the complexities are no worse than the worst optimal, score optimal. If not worse than the worst acceptable, it's acceptable. Any solutions with lower complexities are naive/basic. Then describe the new approach in the result form after choosing "Other" and choose the assessed optimality. Note that the optimality is downgraded if _either_ the time or space complexity is worse. For example, if the worst listed Optimal solution has O(n) time and space, and the worst listed Acceptable solution has O(n^2) time and O(n) space, a solution with O(n*log(n)) time and O(n) space would be Acceptable. A solution with O(n^2) time and O(n^2) space would be Naive/Basic. 

If a candidate describes multiple approaches, choose the one which they made the most progress on coding. Sometimes a candidate will describe an approach before coding and then implement a different approach. Ask the candidate to describe their new approach, and then use the implemented approach for the ranking. 

A candidate's code can also be considered part of the description of an approach, although it is almost always in their interests to describe their approach verbally in case any key elements are missing and you cannot be sure of the viability of the approach. 

If a candidate's approach description lacks detail that affects its optimality, but not its viability, you should assume the worst reasonable approach. For example, if their approach requires them to produce the intersection of two collections, you should assume they will use nested loops rather than sets and set intersection, unless the variables have already been declared as sets. 

If the candidate did not at any point describe an approach which may have worked, and the approach is not clearly viable from the code, then regardless of implementation progress choose **No viable solution** . 

### Note about the 'No viable solution' selection 

Select this option only when the candidate did not at any point describe or implement anything that was on the right track to solve the problem. 

If the candidate did not describe the approach in full, determine if the elements missing from their description could be written off as implementation details or edge cases that require relatively minor fixes. If so, the approach is probably a viable one. 

Every questions has its own quirks, so make sure to check the pitfall section of the questions guides for 

characteristics of a non-viable approach. If you are unsure on whether a "missing element" is an 

**implementation detail** or an **element of a viable approach** and the guide is not clear, please leave a QC note explaining your rationale and file a content form requesting an explanation for that case. A good rule of thumb is, if the problem will appear in testing and be easily fixable, then it likely is an implementation details. For example, a solution to Log Analytics Q2 which is missing sorting, or a DFS implementation missing a visited collection. 

Note that the viability of the candidate’s approach is independent of whether they successfully implemented any of it. 

### Approach Checkboxes: 

- Thoroughly planned and examined approaches 

- Inadequate planning of approach 

- Asked clarifying questions 

Asking clarifying questions to make sure the problem is correctly understood is a positive signal 

## Programming Problems: Completeness 

A candidate's **approach** is an articulated plan or description of an algorithm. 

A candidate's **solution** is the implementation of an approach. 

Completeness is a measure of how correct the candidate's solution was. This typically is the state at the end of the interview, though it should be considered from the point where the candidate made the most progress during the exercise. If the state chosen is not from the end of the interview, it must be explained what happened in Coding Thoughts, usually this is when candidates delete working code when debugging. 

Apart from with **No implementation** and **Fully working** , this leaves room for interpretation, but we try to be as consistent as possible. 

When scoring borderline trivial or mostly-working solutions, please leave a QC note explaining the reasoning behind your decision. In these cases, it is important to have someone from the QC team to have a second look at it. Failing to do so may result in a Take Note. 

### What if the candidate discussed a viable approach but went to implement a non-viable one? 

In that case, select the viable approach they stated and score the code as if that was progressing towards that solution. That may mean their complete code solving the wrong problem could be for example optimal and have trivial (or partial) implementation, rather than non-viable approach. 

### <u>Completeness Options:</u> 

No implementation 

The candidate identified a reasonable approach, but ran out of time before writing any meaningful code. Writing only pseudocode/notes is included in this. If the candidate wrote syntactically valid code in comments (even if in a different language than selected), this still counts towards completeness.

- Re-using code from a previous question’s solution without adding anything meaningful towards solving the current problem. 

Creating function stubs and initialising a few variables, but no actual logic.

### General Guidance 

If the candidate ran out of time after giving a reasonable approach, select "No Implementation." If they wrote some useful code, select "Partial." 

### Partial 

A partial solution is between a "Trivial implementation" and a "Mostly working" solution. 

Examples of partial solutions include code which makes meaningful progress, but either no test cases pass or only a few trivial test cases pass. They may be passing one or two test cases, but be hard-coded to do so. 

It is obvious that significant work remains to arrive at a fully working solution. 

### Mostly working 

There are two definitions of a mostly working solution. 

If the solution fits either one, it is considered to be mostly working. 

1. The candidate is clearly within five minutes of a fully-working solution, without moving the handholding level up. 

2. The solution works in the general sense even if it doesn't run, however, it may not cover an edge case or two. When it works, it is not a result of hard-coding. 

**Note** : Some candidates will continue to work on the problem after the interview. Any performance in the time after the interview cannot influence the scoring of the solution when time ended. This includes a solution that was Partial but the candidate actually did finish it within 5-minutes. 

### Fully working 

The solution is considered fully-working if it satisfies the following: 

- The solution is written in a function and returns the output. 

- The function works completely without any bugs, and all the test cases pass (including producing the expected behaviour for any additional edge cases outside the provided ones). 

- The function returns the same result when run multiple times on the same input and return the correct result when running multiple sequential tests (say, due to global variable use or similar), the Interview Engineer should also point that out and ask them to fix it (please consult the handholding page). That's not to say they can't use global variables for storing data, but their function should ensure that they are reset between runs so that the function returns the correct result when run multiple times. 

Note that the return data can be in any form the candidate chooses. As long as the output is correct, consistent, unambiguous, and works for all potential valid inputs, it is acceptable. It should be in the same rough form as our expected output; for instance if the expected output maps pairs of students to lists of shared courses, then the candidate's output should have some data structure that directly relates those two together such as a map or a list of `(student, student, courses)` tuples. It would not be acceptable to return a tree structure mapping `student -> (map of student -> list)`. 

If the return type is ugly or difficult to work with, you may also address that in coding style section. 

The candidate could also use out parameters, update pass-by-reference parameters, create a custom class with the data in it, or return some kind of generator function, but if this is non-idiomatic for the language you may want to call this out in the coding style section. This does not include storing the data in a global variable instead of returning. 

Some candidates in Object-Oriented languages may chose to create a custom class to represent the whole solution. This could involve using the constructor or a function to generate the data and storing the data in fields on the class, or taking part of the input as a constructor parameter and the rest in a method. This is ok, as long as it satisfies the other requirements (it is especially worth checking that the solution will run correctly when called on the same input multiple times or with different inputs). 

If the candidate's code passes the test cases but relies on some undefined behaviour (such as out-of-bounds memory accesses) and you can't break it during or after the interview, score it as fully-working but note it in the code style section. 

### Additional requirements 

The Interview Engineer should not move on to the next question without the code meeting the following additional requirements. However, if the Interview Engineer fails to enforce any of these additional requirements before moving on to the next question, then the candidate's code would still be considered fully working. The Interview Engineer should expect a Guidelines Take Note from QC though. 

If the candidate does not have time to do all of these at the end of the interview, their code would still be considered fully working and this would _not_ result in a Guidelines Take Note for the Interview Engineer. 

- The function accepts the input in a format provided. 

If the candidate's code modifies the input then you should tell them to make a copy of the input. 

### Completeness Checkboxes 

Did not account for edge cases 

- Note: This is meant to be selected primarily with partial or mostly working completeness, as it's intended to differentiate solutions because edge cases weren't handled vs. the implementation nearing completion. 

## Handholding 

The Handholding score is determined by the amount of guidance and hints given by the Interview Engineer to the candidate. 

The Handholding score has four options: 

- None 

- Minimal 

- Moderate 

- Heavy 

The Handholding score is determined by all of the handholding given, amalgamated. 

The handholding score impacts the candidate's overall score. That is, the more handholding given, the worse the candidate's overall score is. 

See the "Handholding" guide for what is considered handholding and how to choose when to handhold. 

### **What counts as handholding** 

When the candidate does not use the suggestions provided by the Interview Engineer, it is considered handholding in the write up. 

Consider this akin to the amount of time spent holding someone's hand as they cross a tight rope. 

If you provide handholding that the candidate did not need or did not use in their solution, it still counts as handholding and should be scored appropriately. 

If you provide **incorrect** hints or advice, do **not** score it as handholding. This would be considered an interviewer error and candidates should not be penalized for such reason. 

### <u>Handholding Options:</u> 

See the handholding guide, which contains explanations of each level. 

###### **None** 

###### **Minimal (Handholding)** 

###### **Moderate (Handholding)** 

###### **Heavy (Handholding)** 

### Handholding Checkboxes 

- Helped formulate approach 

- Helped with syntax errors 

- Helped identify errors in approach Helped identify bugs or edge cases Helped fix bugs 

### Adding up handholding 

There is some natural grey area here. For example, if a candidate writes their own implementation but has 20 bugs and needs help fixing every one, at that point it’s probably not just "major help finding bugs", it’s "major help with solution", and therefore it should be scored as Heavy. 

It is common for an Interview Engineer to choose a score of "Moderate", for example, after giving multiple "Minimal" guidances. 

When in doubt, put whatever you think is the closest match to the criteria above; you can always ask for input from QC in the IVE QC category in Vanilla or leave comments about your concern in the **QC Notes** as well. 

The "Thoughts" section is a great place to describe what the candidate failed to do without help. This can justify any score and helps someone doing Quality Control (QC) to help you to get more in line with Karat scoring standards. Choose the closest match and **ask for feedback in the notes to QC** if you are not sure. 

## Programming Problems: Debugging 

This field is intended to denote the amount of debugging that the candidate had to do on the problem. The idea is to differentiate between candidates who write fully working code immediately vs. those who need to repeatedly address problems with correctness. The bar for Minimal debugging should be anything beyond two or three compiler-type issues. Any logical mistakes constitute debugging. Heavy debugging should denote the candidate spending most of the interview debugging rather than writing code. 

### <u>Debugging Options:</u> 

**None (Debugging)** : The candidate’s code worked on the first go or they only had to fix some small syntax errors / typos. 

Minimal (Debugging): The candidate had to track down and fix one or maybe two small logical errors. 

Moderate (Debugging): The candidate had to spend a few minutes on a larger bug or two 

**Heavy (Debugging)** : The candidate spent a significant amount of time debugging a solution that wasn’t working as intended 

### Debugging Checkboxes 

- Struggled with syntax errors 

- Struggled with logic errors Off-by-one errors 

## Programming Problems: Time and Space Complexities 

For each coding question that is completed, the candidate should be asked what the time and space complexities are of their solution. If available direct the candidate to do this in the terms defined at the bottom of the question prompt. 

If you are not sure what the time and space complexities of their solution are, **call this out in the notes to QC** on an interview. 

The available options are as follows: 

- If the candidate gives an incorrect time or space complexity, move on. 

- If the candidate gives a time or space complexity which is ambiguous, such as `O(n)` for a function with multiple inputs, ask the candidate to clarify what `n` is. Having to ask for this clarification does not impact the score. 

A candidate's response must be clear but does not need to be in perfect Big O syntax. For example, a complexity of `O(2n)` should be described as `O(n)` but we consider this correct. 

Similarly, if a candidate describes complexity as "constant in terms of the size of the input", this should be considered the same as `O(1)`. 

### <u>Complexity Options:</u> 

**Correctly identified both** : The candidate correctly identified both the time and space complexities. 

**Only identified time** : The candidate correctly identified time complexity but incorrectly identified space complexity. 

**Only identified space** : The candidate correctly identified space complexity but incorrectly identified time complexity. 

**Incorrectly identified both** : The candidate incorrectly identified both the time and space complexities. 

**Was not asked** : The Interview Engineer did not ask the candidate to identify time and space complexities. 

In the event that only time complexity was asked, a correct response should mark "Correctly identified both" and an incorrect response should mark "Incorrectly identified both". 

If time or space complexities were asked, they should be scored accordingly, despite IVE's errors. In other words, if a solution is found to be mostly-working after time and space complexities were discussed, the candidate should still be given credit for the discussion. 

## Programming Questions: Thoughts 

The main scores for the entire interview are generated by the score labels for each question. The Thoughts section for each question should have a few sentences describing in detail how the candidate performed. **It should align with the scoring labels.** 

For example, if handholding was scored as "Heavy" then the thoughts section should mention what hints were given that merited a score of "Heavy." 

If there were bugs in the code (fixed or not), they should be mentioned here as well. Thoughts should be written up for each part of the technical question reached.

Required items to include:

## Code Style 

### Code Style: Scoring 

The Code Style scoring provides an indication of how well the candidate coded, as much as possible ignoring their method of finding valid approaches to the coding problems. 

Scores should reflect the overall coding ability, and are often based on the Interview Engineer's judgment. If a candidate's coding proficiency appears to differ depending on the part of the problem at hand, choose the closest option to what you consider the average and optionally describe the situation in the "Thoughts" section. 

There are <u>three key metrics that we consider for Code Style:</u> 

- Syntax Style Fluency 

For each of these there are checkboxes. These checkboxes aim to give a bit of color to the hard score. They are often derived from common "Thoughts" in write ups. 

### Code Style: Syntax 

The Syntax score is designed to show the client a candidate's proficiency and apparent familiarity with the programming language they chose. 

What helps us know this is the following: 

Can they implement simple code structures without help or mistakes? 

Can they implement more complex code structures without help or mistakes? 

- Is their code idiomatic? 

This does not cover typing errors. 

An Interview Engineer who is familiar with a language is more likely to know whether code is idiomatic. However, in many languages it is quite clear to an experienced Interview Engineer whether code is idiomatic enough. 

Examples of non-idiomatic code: 

- Using integers 1 and 0 or strings "True" and "False" in a language which supports booleans. 

- Using a custom "default" value in a language which supports null or None where appropriate 

### <u>Syntax Options:</u> 

**Syntax Insufficient data** : The candidate for some reason did not code or wrote not enough code to feel comfortable making a call on their syntax. For example, they decided to leave the interview before the coding section or wrote a trivial solution to the first question with only a few lines of code that was never compiled. 

**Pseudocode only / unable** : The candidate demonstrates almost no proficiency in their chosen language. This usually manifests as either: 

- A candidate writes just English sentences. 

- A candidate writes code which never compiles or runs without help. 

**Many errors** : The candidate's code includes many errors. A few "large" errors may also put a candidate in this category. A large error may for example be very incorrect code to perform a simple task such as printing a debug statement or initializing a variable. 

**Few errors** : Most candidates fall into this category. The candidate can get work done in their chosen language, but they require help from the compiler output, the Interview Engineer or the web at some points. 

**Near perfect** : The candidate demonstrates strong proficiency in their language of choice. They quickly fix minor syntax errors and understand the compiler output with ease. They use idiomatic code. 

### Code Style: Style 

The Style rating is intended to give a client signal of the quality of the code that a candidate will produce in terms of maintainability. Candidates are encouraged to aim for completeness, and so we do not expect code to be as neat as it might be in a production codebase. That said, strong candidates write code which is neat and easy to follow, as well as code which it is easy to add features to. 

High quality production code usually includes comments. We do not expect these in the interview situation due to time constraints. 

The style should refer to the final state of the code, for example if the code used poor variable names, but the candidate fixed it, do not select "poor variable names" (though it might be worth commenting that they were poor and then fixed, it gives a useful signal). 

### <u>Style Options:</u> 

**Style Insufficient data** : The candidate for some reason did not code or wrote not enough code to feel comfortable making a call on their style. For example, they decided to leave the interview before the coding section or the solution did not have enough code to determine if best practices would be followed or if it would end up being easy or hard to maintain. 

**Spaghetti code** : Code that flagrantly violates the principles of structured, procedural programming. Extremely difficult, if not impossible, to debug and maintain. The code has a tangled and arbitrary program flow. 

**Hard to understand/maintain** : The code shows some characteristics of easy to understand code, but also multiple characteristics of spaghetti code. 

**Easy to understand/maintain** : Mostly average or crystal clear. This is typically the default option if no checkboxes are selected. 

**Beautifully written** : This should be selected if the candidate wrote code with exceptional high quality. "Beautifully written" code is usually concise and clear. 

- All variable names are easy to understand (except for maybe one or two). 

- Helper functions are pure and easily testable. 

- The approach as described is clearly understandable from reading the code. 

- The code style is consistent. 

### Style Checkboxes 

The available checkboxes give insight into common causes of hard to maintain code in interviews: 

- Did not use helper methods when appropriate 

- Implementation is more complex than what the approach calls for 

- Numerous variable identifiers were confusing, misleading, or impaired readability 

   - Used when there are multiple instances of poor variable identifiers being used. This includes highly abbreviated (one or two characters) names, non-descriptive names such as `map`, `set,` or `list`. However, keep in mind that in an interview setting naming typically takes a backseat to problem solving so borderline variable identifiers that are still understandable with context are okay. Likewise, one or two poor names on their own do not constitute this checkbox. The idea here is to call out a candidate that demonstrates a pattern or habit of not following proper naming conventions. 

###### Used magic numbers 

- Used hardcoded numbers or values without explanation or understanding. For example, subtracting 1 from the returned result because guess and check said so, or using "FOOBAR" or -1 as a special default or stop value rather than None/null/nil. 

- This could also mean hardcoding sizes of a sample input or otherwise writing a solution that would only work for a specific input. 

###### Inconsistent indentation/spacing 

- This is intended for candidates that demonstrate a pattern of poor whitespace practices. This could mean inconsistent indentation, huge blocks of whitespace, or no whitespace usage at all. It is not intended for one or two mis-indented blocks of code. 

###### Used global variables 

If a candidate relied on global variables when it could otherwise be avoided this checkbox is applicable. This is not meant to capture use of member variables of a class in an OOP language. 

Had excessive duplicated or repeated code 

- This is intended for candidates that copy and paste parts of their code rather than using a helper method when it would have been beneficial to do so. 

- This could also encompass not using a temporary variable for repeated deep data structure access like `map[key][array_index]` instead of `value = map[key][array_index]` and then using `value`. 

Had unnecessary deeply nested code 

- This is intended for candidates that use excessively nested logic to the point that it detriments understanding of the code. There is no hard limit to this as it is dependent on language and conventions, but 4+ levels of nesting is when it may become applicable. 

### Code Style: Fluency 

The fluency score gives an indication of how quickly and comfortably a candidate can write code, given an idea. 

In some ways this relates to typing speed, but more often it is an indicator of the candidate's familiarity with their language of choice, programming constructs and ability to translate an abstract idea to code. It is NOT strictly a measure of how quickly a candidate solves one of the programming questions or debugs logic errors in their code. For example, is the candidate able to effortlessly and swiftly write code or do they hesitate and need to think through syntax/code structure frequently? 

### <u>Fluency Options:</u> 

**Fluency Insufficient data** : The candidate for some reason did not code or wrote not enough code to feel comfortable making a call on their fluency. For example, they decided to leave the interview before the coding section. If they otherwise only wrote a small amount of code and generally performed poorly in the interview then "Limited" is probably the correct option to select. 

**Limited** : The candidate has major or constant trouble translating thoughts to written code. They struggle or are unable to produce meaningfully-working code in a reasonable timeframe. 

**Proficient** : The candidate is able to write code, but has some trouble efficiently doing so or simply works at a steady pace. 

**Native** : The candidate produces code without any problems. They easily and swiftly write code that illustrates their current thought. 

### Fluency Checkboxes 

- Tested incrementally during implementation 

- Tested additional test cases unprompted

## Communication 

### Communication: Scoring 

Communication is a key part of being a software engineer in a team. Only a small part of a candidate's true communication skills can be assessed in our interview, but we want to give the client the information we have. 

Candidates are scored against four communication-related metrics: 

- Clarity of Speech 

- Quality of Explanation Comprehension 

- Interaction 

### Communication Checkboxes 

For each metric, there are optional checkboxes that can be used to capture rarer events. To illustrate, Interaction is a required criteria that can always be reasonably judged -- were the candidate’s interactions with you polite and professional, or not? The optional criteria in the Interaction bucket include things like 'Became Frustrated' if the candidate became visibly frustrated, or 'Argumentative/Defensive' for when the candidate is always trying to fight any suggestions of improvement made by the Interview Engineer. Neither of these happen very often, but when they do happen they are very important (in these cases, negative) signals that we want to call out to clients. 

### Communication: Clarity of Speech 

How well the Interview Engineer understood the words the candidate spoke. 

The candidate's speech does not have to be perfect - all that matters is how easily you could understand it. For example, a candidate might have a slight-to-moderate accent but still be perfectly understandable. 

Internet connection issues can obviously impact the signal quality, which in turn impacts how clearly a candidate's speech comes across. Interview Engineers should, as best as possible, attempt to grade this criterion independent of the signal quality. 

### <u>Clarity of Speech Options:</u> 

**Easy to understand** : few or no clarifying questions along the lines of "I didn't understand; could you repeat what you just said?" 

**Some challenges understanding** : Some clarifying questions are needed. 

**Difficult to understand** : candidate has to be repeatedly asked to reiterate the words they were saying. 

### Clarity of Speech Checkboxes 

- **Spoke too quickly** : the candidate spoke so quickly that it made them difficult to understand. 

- **Mumbled** : the candidate mumbled at various points in the interview. 

- **Language proficiency significantly impaired communication** : the candidate's English proficiency made understanding them considerably difficult to the point that would affect day to day communication with them. 

- **Poor audio connection impaired communication** : The candidate's computer setup was improper for the interview. Avoid scoring down the "clarity of speech" field for this as it's usually out of the candidate's control if the audio connection negatively affected communication. 

### Communication: Quality of Explanation 

This dimension deals with how well the candidate explained their thoughts, independent of the 

`Clarity of Speech` of the candidate. 

Does the candidate choose their words well, structure their ideas in an understandable way, and explain their ideas clearly and concisely? 

To illustrate the distinction between Clarity of Speech and Quality of Explanation: 

Do you understand the words they are saying well (indicating good `Clarity of Speech`) yet find yourself asking multiple clarifying questions (indicating poor `Quality of Explanation`)? 

### <u>Quality of Explanation Options:</u> 

**Organized, succinct explanations** : Candidate structures their ideas and explanations clearly and concisely, making them notably easy to understand. They begin by setting appropriate context before diving into details. 

**Clear explanations** : Candidate explains concepts clearly and effectively. Explanations may occasionally be unorganized or not fully contextualized, but are still easy to follow. 

**Some challenges understanding explanations** : Occasional clarifying questions are needed. 

**Difficult to understand explanations** : Struggles to explain ideas. Gets lost in details, or fails to explain context. 

### Quality of Explanation Checkboxes 

- **Rambled** : The candidate repeatedly took longer than necessary to explain ideas. 

- **Digressed** : The candidate repeatedly digressed into irrelevant details. 

- Did not ask questions when necessary 

- Did not build off of prompts 

### Communication: Comprehension 

Indicates how well the candidate understood the Interview Engineer. 

- Did the candidate understand what was being asked of them? 

- Did they easily incorporate the Interview Engineer's feedback? 

- Did the Interview Engineer need to repeat themselves multiple times? (NB: This only tracks general, nontechnical comprehension. If a candidate understands you but e.g. doesn’t know what a technical term like ‘priority queue’ means, that should not affect this Comprehension score.) 

### <u>Comprehension Options:</u> 

**Easily understood Interview Engineer** : Candidate clearly understood the Interview Engineer all or almost all of the time, and easily incorporated any feedback. 

**Some challenges understanding Interview Engineer** : The Interview Engineer occasionally needed to repeat themselves. 

**Difficulty understanding Interview Engineer** : Candidate had significant struggles understanding the Interview Engineer. The Interview Engineer frequently needed to repeat themselves. 

### Comprehension Checkboxes 

- **Did not answer questions** : The candidate's response to questions showed a fundamental misunderstanding of what the Interview Engineer said or was asking for. 

- **Did not adjust to Interview Engineer’s feedback** : The candidate did not understand, and therefore failed to properly implement, hints or feedback from the Interview Engineer. 

### Communication: Interaction 

Indicates whether the candidate interacted with the Interview Engineer in a polite, professional manner. Most interactions with candidates go well, but when they don't, it's an important signal to call out. 

### <u>Interaction Options:</u> 

**Notable interpersonal skills** : The candidate actively engages with the Interview Engineer and demonstrates enthusiasm for the role and/or the interview itself. Candidate is uniformly polite and friendly, and may proactively ask for clarification or establish parameters of the interview and exercise. 

**Smooth interaction** : The candidate behaves appropriately and is polite and professional throughout the interview. Candidate listens to instructions and asks appropriate questions. 

**Minor problems with interaction** : Candidate was rarely impolite or unprofessional. Even if it happens once, that should be enough to earn this lower notch. 

**Poor interaction** : The candidate was repeatedly impolite or unprofessional. Anything more than once should probably fall under this category, given how rare poor interactions are, and how candidates are generally already trying to be on their best behavior. 

### Interaction Checkboxes 

- **Argumentative/Defensive** : The candidate was overly defensive or argumentative when their actions were questioned or a mistake was pointed out. 

- **Became frustrated** : The candidate became visibly frustrated during the interview. 

- **Interrupted Interview Engineer** : The candidate repeatedly interrupted the Interview Engineer. 

- **Unenthusiastic / disengaged** : The candidate showed active disinterest in the interview process and/or content. 

- Late to interview 

   - More than three minutes past the start of the interview. 

Poor location for interview 

For example, locations with large amount of background noise such as libraries or coffee shops. 

- Unprepared for interview 

- Used excessive profanity 

   - Incidental profanity is generally not a cause for concern. However, if it bothers you then this checkbox is generally applicable. 

Did not follow Interview Engineer's instructions

## Discussion and Analysis Questions (DAQs) 

Only certain clients request use of the Discussion and Analysis questions. Sometimes Discussion and Analysis questions are used instead of the Project Discussion; sometimes the Project Discussion is shortened and followed by a short set of Discussion and Analysis Questions. Consult the client script for details. 

Discussion and Analysis Questions are sets of technical questions with straightforward answers. On the results form, you can indicate correctness by selecting appropriate checkboxes. 

### Discussion and Analysis Questions: Thoughts 

Please include some information about the candidate’s answers to each question in these Discussion and Analysis Questions, regardless of whether they answered correctly or not. The purpose is to give the hiring manager some additional insight into the quality of the answers the candidate provides as this has been requested to assist them in making hiring decisions.

Explain that the candidate did not answer any of the questions correctly

Mention that you provided hints. You should not provide hints on Discussion and Analysis questions, but just in case it happens.

## Offline Coding Challenge (Offline Code) 

### Offline Code: Scoring 

In the results form, you’ll be asked to grade their level of understanding of their implementation, as well as whether they were able to identify the time and space complexities of their implementation. 

In some cases, their explanation will be so poor that you aren’t able to discern whether their time and space complexities analysis was correct. In this case, explain your uncertainty in the Thoughts section, and err on the side of marking it as correct. 

### <u>Offline Code Level of Understanding Options:</u> 

Note that these are not prescriptive; some candidates may show varying levels of understanding of their approach and code, so use your best judgement to determine which option best fits their performance. 

Minimal: The candidate struggled to explain their approach or what their code did. They did not have much if any insight into other ways to approach the problem. 

Moderate: The candidate gave a brief overview of their approach, and explained their code line by line. They may have another approach in mind. 

Above-average: The candidate gave a good description of their approach, and explained their code in pieces rather than just reading the code. If there was another approach they could have taken, they explain it well and describe the trade-offs. Otherwise, they explain why their solution is optimal. They may share some insight into the structure of their code. 

Significant: The candidate succinctly explains their approach and how their code solves the problem. They explain how they structured their code clearly, as well as any trade-offs in their approach or code structure. 

Not enough info: The candidate either did not answer the question or their explanations were completely unintelligible. 

### Offline Code: Thoughts 

For the written part of the results form, you don't need to go into too much depth. Describe in a few sentences what they said to you at a high level, how well they explained it, and how well they appeared to understand what they were talking about.

## Written Feedback to the Candidate 

###### **WARNING** 

###### **The written feedback should be brief and in bullet-point format.** 

Please provide a few bullet points of feedback. This feedback is not sent directly to candidate, but passed on by recruiters when candidates reach out for feedback. It is recommended to leave 1-2 things that were done well and 1-2 areas/suggestions for improvement. 

###### **Do not reveal any information about how the candidate performed in relation to other candidates, or any information about whether you think they "passed" or "failed" or that they did great or not overall. Remember, we don’t make decisions about candidates.** 

The written feedback should be a brief summary of _what_ happened during the interview. Be cautious about passing judgment on _how_ they worked, lest it come across as dismissive of their style or past experiences. Unless a candidate explicitly asks for advice on _how_ they can improve, it’s best to give them observational information and let them figure out what to do with it. 

### Example Items to Provide as Feedback 

You may find the following list helpful. It is compiled and summarized from written feedback that Interview Engineers have previously given internal candidates: 

#### Introduction Example 

Be prepared to give a one minute elevator pitch about yourself and your technical background. 

#### Project Discussion Example 

Be prepared to talk about a technical project. 

- Focus on a technical challenge and how you handled it. 

- Non-technical aspects aren't as important in an interview setting. Ask yourself, "What skills do I want to highlight to a potential employer?" 

#### Coding Exercise Example 

Practice, practice, practice! 

Think through the algorithm and think about code design ahead of time. 

- Start by writing the simplest parts, like the method signature(s), to get comfortable. 

- Identify straightforward solutions first, and then think about how to improve it. 

   - Think about this before writing code, since the interview will move on once you have working code. Completely working code is better than partially working code, regardless of approach. 

Always verbalize your approach, especially key decisions and reasoning. 

   - Your Interview Engineer can read code and will ask questions as necessary. 

- Discuss trade-offs of potential solutions: your Interview Engineer will steer you toward what they want to see in a solution. 

- Ask clarifying questions, the Interview Engineer might be intentionally vague to see if you make assumptions. 

- Break the problem down, break the code into smaller functions. 

- Choose a language best suited for the problem at hand; choose a language where you can shine! 

- Use the runtime environment and interpreter, if relevant to your language, to your advantage. 

- Brush up on big-O and be able to analyze time and space complexity of common data structure operations and code flow. 

- Don't forget about edge cases. Propose additional test cases to check all boundary conditions and increase confidence in the correctness of your code. 

- Brush up on standard libraries of languages you're familiar with, you will mostly likely need to use data structures in your solution. 

- Keeping a dialogue going with the Interview Engineer while coding can help the Interview Engineer better follow along and guide you, if necessary.

## Candidate Decides to Terminate Interview Early 

If the candidate opts to end the interview early, we should mention this in the Thoughts paragraph in the section in which they decided to terminate the interview. 

Be careful to keep the tone neutral and do not speculate as to why they decided to end the interview early, just state the fact that they did. 

### Example - Candidate terminates interview during the first coding question 

#### Q1 Thoughts 

Jill proposed a solution to iterate through the input to identify the first zero and last zero as the top-left and bottom-right coordinates respectively. She struggled to translate her approach into code. She then opted to end the interview twenty minutes early. 

## When would I _not_ need to submit a write-up? 

If the candidate chooses to end the interview early, a write-up is most likely still needed. If the interview ends early due to issues that are out of the candidate's control, such as technical problems, then we will reschedule the interview and you may not need to submit a write-up.
